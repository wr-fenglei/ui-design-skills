# Design Principles

## 1. Diversity before refinement

Early exploration should maximize **structural diversity**. A good exploration set varies on multiple axes:
- metaphor
- silhouette
- composition
- color strategy
- material
- dimensionality
- texture
- motion implication

A weak exploration set keeps one layout and changes hue or surface rendering.

## 2. One thesis per concept

Every icon needs a one-sentence thesis that explains why the form exists.

Examples:
- “Two opposing masses create intelligence through the void between them.”
- “Independent geometric primitives become a system through tension and overlap.”
- “A soft translucent organism suggests adaptive, living intelligence.”
- “Discrete pixels condense into a directional signal.”
- “A tactile architectural object contains a luminous core.”

The thesis should survive even if colors change.

## 3. Negative space is a design material

Empty regions can carry:
- identity
- motion
- separation
- hierarchy
- hidden symbols
- visual breathing room

Do not fill space merely to make the icon look rich.

## 4. Material must have a reason

Glass, metal, stone, gel, paper, pixel glow, and flat ink imply different product personalities.

Choose material because it reinforces the concept, not because it looks expensive.

## 5. Avoid AI-default aesthetics

Common failure modes:
- generic glossy gradient blob
- excessive glow
- arbitrary translucent layers
- pill-shaped everything
- soft shadows everywhere
- undifferentiated pastel palettes
- many small details with no hierarchy

## 6. References calibrate; they do not dictate

Use references to answer:
- Is this direction too close to an existing one?
- Is the set broad enough?
- Is the level of polish appropriate?
- Is the design thesis legible?

Do not copy a reference's exact silhouette, composition, or signature detail.
